The Price of an Ordinary Morning

We talk about inflation in percentages and charts. But what happens when an economic idea enters the smallest rituals of an ordinary day?

By Umeed-e-Sahar Editorial · 8 min read

There is a strange way an economic idea becomes real. It does not arrive first as a graph. It arrives when the tea seller quietly changes the number on the board, when the bus conductor asks for a little more, when the same handful of groceries occupies a larger share of a week's income.

In Pakistan, the word inflation can feel almost too large to carry. It belongs to television tickers and economic reports. But the experience of it is intimate. It sits on a kitchen counter. It waits at a petrol pump. It appears in the arithmetic people perform in their heads before ordering lunch.

Behind every statistic is a life being lived.

The morning has an economy

Consider a student leaving home in the morning. There is a bus fare, a cup of tea, perhaps a notebook bought on the way. None of these transactions feels like a macroeconomic event. Together, however, they describe a small economy.

This is why economics is worth bringing closer to ordinary life. A percentage can tell us that prices rose. It cannot, on its own, tell us what a person stopped buying, what a family postponed, or what a student began walking instead of riding.

Numbers are stories in disguise

The task of a good economic explanation is not to make the reader admire the number. It is to help the reader understand what the number means when it leaves the page and enters someone's day.
